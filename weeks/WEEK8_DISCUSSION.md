1. Which hyperparameters did you choose to tune, and why did you prioritise them?

The hyperparameters I prioritised this round were those that most directly control how the Gaussian Process (GP) surrogate transfers information across the input space and how the acquisition function turns that posterior into the next query. Concretely, I tuned: (i) Matérn smoothness ν per function (0.5 for rough/noisy surfaces such as F1/F2; 1.5–2.5 for smoother ones such as F3/F8); (ii) ARD length-scale bounds, capped at (0.01, 10) for lower-dimensional functions and audited more carefully on F8 so that genuinely longer scales are not artificially crushed; (iii) a learnable WhiteKernel noise term retained for noisy F2 (and considered for F4), rather than forced on every function; (iv) GP noise floor α; (v) acquisition parameters — EI ξ near 0.01 for exploit-ready functions and UCB κ lowered toward ~0.5–1.5 once a reliable incumbent exists (F4, F8), while keeping higher κ only where structure is still uncertain; and (vi) the trust-region radius around the incumbent, which I now treat as an explicit hyperparameter because Week 7 showed that “micro-steps” away from exact best coordinates on F6/F7 lost performance.

I prioritised these over neural-network capacity hyperparameters because, with roughly 17–47 observations per function, the decision that matters is where to sample next under calibrated uncertainty. Length scales and noise govern that uncertainty; ξ/κ and the trust-region radius govern how aggressively we leave a known good region. Maximising neural-net prediction accuracy is secondary when one query per week is the real budget.

2. How has hyperparameter tuning changed your query strategy compared to earlier rounds?

Earlier rounds often trusted a global EI/UCB optimum too readily. With poorly constrained kernels, empty regions inflated posterior variance and pulled candidates toward boundaries or away from established basins. After capping length scales, enabling WhiteKernel where noise is real, applying log-y on F5, and decaying κ as evidence accumulates, the fitted GPs became trustworthy enough to exploit. The strategy therefore shifted from “maximise acquisition over the whole box” to “search inside a trust region around the incumbent, ranked by posterior mean / low-ξ EI,” with an explicit trust gate: if the surrogate is untrustworthy (F1’s near-zero radiation readings), I refuse GP exploit and use space-filling / soft-signal probes instead.

Practically, better-tuned models make me lean more on predictions where diagnostics agree (F3–F8), but not everywhere. On F2, noise modelling stopped me treating every small drop as a new local story; I return to the exact W5 best rather than chasing another exploratory jump. On F6/F7, tuning the step size (trust radius) downward means returning to exact best coordinates after Week 7’s failed micro-steps. Exploration is reserved for regions where the model fails a trust check (F1’s second warm lobe), not for inventing sensitivity from noise.

3. Which tuning methods did you apply, and what trade-offs did you notice?

Kernel continuous hyperparameters (length scales, WhiteKernel noise) are tuned by maximising log marginal likelihood inside `gpr.fit()` with multiple random restarts — not by hand-setting each length scale. Structural choices — Matérn ν, whether to include WhiteKernel, whether to log-transform y, EI vs UCB — were compared on a small manual grid using weekly diagnostics (best-so-far trajectory, length-scale stability, whether queries drift to corners). Acquisition ξ/κ and trust-region radii were adjusted manually against those diagnostics.

I did not run Hyperband or a second-level Bayesian optimisation over the BO hyperparameters this week: with one query per function per week and modest n, the cost of a large outer search is not justified, and LML already provides a principled inner fit. The trade-off is clear. Marginal-likelihood + a small structural grid is transparent and appropriate for this budget, but it can miss better combinations and risks stale hand rules (for example, freezing a dimension that later becomes informative). Manual trust-region radii are interpretable, yet they must be re-audited each week so they do not fossilise around an outdated best.

4. As your dataset grows past ~16 points, what model limitations became clearer?

Several limitations are now concrete. First, small-n model selection is unstable: ARD rankings can flip after a few new points, so I treat “inactive dimension” locks as provisional and re-check them. Second, irrelevant or weakly relevant features only become identifiable with enough contrast — F8 needed dozens of points before length scales started to separate; F5’s log transform changed which inputs looked sensitive. Third, diminishing returns around known optima are real: F5 repeatedly re-hits ~3744 on the ridge, F8’s recent gains are on the order of 0.001, and F7’s micro-steps underperformed the exact incumbent. Noise testing matters here — a tiny F8 improvement can still be signal, whereas chasing sub-noise differences on F2/F4 wastes queries. Fourth, when the GP has no measurable signal (F1), more data alone does not legitimise exploitation; a trust gate must route those functions to exploration heuristics instead of a confidently wrong posterior.

5. How would you apply hyperparameter tuning techniques to larger datasets or future ML/AI projects?

As n grows, I would keep LML multi-restart fits for GP kernels, but re-run the structural grid (ν, WhiteKernel on/off, transforms) regularly and retire stale constraints. Adaptive trust regions (TuRBO-style) would replace fixed radii. For richer surrogates — random forests, gradient-boosted trees, or neural nets once data are dense enough — I would switch the outer tuner: grid/random search or Successive Halving/Hyperband over capacity and regularisation, allocating expensive full-fidelity training only to promising configurations. In other words, match the tuning method to model cost: cheap, transparent grids for GP acquisition settings; multi-fidelity search for deep models. HEBO-style input/output warping would also generalise the per-function log10 decisions I already use on F5.

6. How does tuning in this black-box setting prepare you to think like a professional ML practitioner?

This capstone forces the same conditions practitioners face: no known ground truth, expensive evaluations, and models that can be confidently wrong. There are always two optimisation problems running at once — improving the unknown objective, and improving the optimiser’s own hyperparameters. Week 7’s lesson was validating assumptions before spending queries: leaving exact best coordinates without a noise-aware justification hurt F6/F7, while a light, low-κ exploit on F8 produced a real (if small) gain. The professional habit is to interrogate the surrogate — length scales, noise, trust metrics — not only the predicted peak, and to match method sophistication to evidence. Fancy acquisition on an untrustworthy GP is not “more advanced”; refusing it on F1 and exploiting tightly where the model is reliable is.
